Radar Abitamo, 5 October 2026.

On 29 September, Spain's Council of Ministers approved two royal decree laws on housing. On 2 October, Congress repealed them. Today, Pedro Sánchez called a general election for 29 November.

Three milestones in one week. And one question nobody has answered well: what happens to my lease?

<div class="alert alert-primary my-4" role="note"><strong>In 30 seconds.</strong> The decrees of 29 September changed lease renewals, notice periods and rent increases. Congress repealed them on 2 October, so they are not in force today. The government expects to approve them again on 6 October, with changes, and there is an election on 29 November.</div>

## What was approved on 29 September

There were two texts. The first, Royal Decree Law 26/2026, was published in the BOE (Spain's official gazette) on 30 September. It bundled several measures: a suspension of evictions of vulnerable people with no alternative housing until 31 December 2030, a limit on speculative purchases by investment entities (only below 70% of market value, until the end of 2028), and tax benefits for renting.

It also hit tenants' wallets directly. For leases whose anniversary fell while the decree was in force, rent could not rise if it already exceeded the cap set by the reference index system, and in every other case the increase could not go above 2%. A tenant up to date on payments could also request an extraordinary extension of up to two years for leases expiring before 2029.

The second, Royal Decree Law 27/2026, was published on 1 October and entered into force the next day. Its goal was stability for primary residence leases. Contracts would renew automatically for five year periods, or seven if the landlord is a legal entity. A landlord would have to give six months' notice not to renew, against two months for the tenant, and would owe compensation of at least twelve months' rent of a comparable home, with exceptions such as the landlord needing the home for personal or family use.

## What happened next

Both decrees needed Congress to ratify them within 30 days. They didn't have to wait that long. On 2 October the PP, Vox and Junts voted against and the chamber repealed them. According to OCU, the second decree was in force that same day, and the BOE published the repeal resolutions on the same date.

In practice, OCU says, the changes stop applying going forward. The Urban Leases Act and Law 12/2023 on the right to housing remain in force. But anything done while the decrees were in force, such as a rent update with an anniversary on 1 October or a court suspension already granted, stays valid. OCU recommends reviewing communications, registered letters, requests and court rulings together with their dates.

## The market didn't wait

Landlords reacted in the meantime. The Observatorio del Alquiler, run by the Fundación Alquiler Seguro, detected 4,290 fewer homes than expected between 28 and 30 September, right as the decrees were announced. The Observatorio itself warns that not all of that drop can be pinned on regulation.

Still, the behaviour is easy to read. When a landlord doesn't know what rules their lease will live under in three months, signing gets pushed back. And when a tenant doesn't know whether their extension exists, they can't plan to move or to stay.

## What comes next

With the election call, parliament is dissolved. The government has announced it will approve the decrees again this Tuesday, 6 October, with some changes, according to minister Ernest Urtasun. Ratification would no longer fall to the full chamber but to the Diputación Permanente, a 69 member body with different arithmetic from the plenary, according to Euronews.

That means the lease changes could come back, but in a different text from the one of 29 September. We don't yet know what it will say. And after the election, a new majority could change direction again.

## What to do in the meantime

If you're a landlord, don't assume the measures will return or that they won't. Keep dated records of every communication with your tenant and check when your lease expires.

If you're a tenant, keep any communication received on 1 or 2 October and get advice before signing any change to your contract.

Either way, what is in your hands is having the contract and every communication properly documented. Rules can change with a vote. Signed paperwork can't.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. The decrees' text may change after the new approval planned for 6 October.*
